Section 4: Overt Channels: Formal Communication in Multi-Agent AI Systems

While steganography provides a mechanism for covert, hidden communication, the majority of collaborative AI work relies on overt, explicit, and standardized channels. To provide a complete understanding of how AI systems interact, it is essential to contrast the secret world of steganography with the structured world of formal communication protocols. These protocols are the bedrock of Multi-Agent Systems (MAS), enabling multiple autonomous agents to work together to solve complex problems. The evolution of these protocols mirrors the increasing sophistication of AI, moving from early academic frameworks to modern standards designed for the era of LLMs.

4.1 The Collaborative Intelligence Paradigm

A Multi-Agent System is a computational framework composed of multiple autonomous AI agents that interact with each other and their environment to achieve common or individual goals.41 This paradigm is powerful because it allows for the decomposition of large, complex problems into smaller, manageable sub-tasks that can be assigned to specialized agents.41 This approach offers several advantages over a single, monolithic AI system, including enhanced modularity, scalability, resilience (as the failure of one agent does not necessarily cripple the entire system), and the ability to integrate agents with diverse expertise.41

For a MAS to function effectively, however, communication is paramount.42 The agents must have a shared understanding of how to exchange information, make requests, report results, and coordinate their actions. Without a common language and set of rules, the system would devolve into a collection of isolated silos, incapable of meaningful collaboration.44 This necessity drives the development and adoption of

agent communication protocols, which provide the standardized syntax, semantics, and pragmatics for inter-agent dialogue.42 These protocols are the foundation that turns a group of individual agents into a cohesive, intelligent collective.

4.2 Protocols for Agentic Dialogue: From KQML to Modern Standards

The history of agent communication protocols shows a clear evolutionary path, reflecting the broader trends in computer science and artificial intelligence. This progression can be seen as analogous to the development of internet standards, moving from early, rigid frameworks to the flexible, service-oriented protocols required by today's distributed, LLM-powered systems.

Historical Foundations: The conceptual groundwork for agent communication was laid in the 1990s with the development of Agent Communication Languages (ACLs). These early protocols were primarily academic and focused on enabling knowledge sharing between well-defined, symbolic AI agents.

  • KQML (Knowledge Query and Manipulation Language): Developed under the aegis of DARPA, KQML was a pioneering protocol that defined a message format and a set of "performatives" (e.g., ask-one, tell, achieve) to express the intent of a message.41 It established the fundamental idea of separating the content of a message from the communicative act it was intended to perform.

  • FIPA-ACL (Foundation for Intelligent Physical Agents - Agent Communication Language): Building on the concepts of KQML, FIPA-ACL emerged as a more formalized and widely adopted standard.41 It provided a more rigorous semantic framework and a broader set of performatives, aiming to ensure interoperability between agents developed by different organizations.41

Modern, LLM-Centric Protocols: The rise of LLMs and cloud-native architectures has created a need for a new generation of protocols that are more flexible, scalable, and better suited for interaction with APIs and external tools. These modern protocols are often designed to be "stacked," with each protocol handling a different layer of the communication problem.

  • MCP (Model Context Protocol): This protocol focuses on standardizing how an agent interacts with its tools and the external world.45 It defines a common interface for agents to connect to APIs, databases, and other data sources. In the analogy of an organization, MCP is the "internal wiki and playbook" that tells an agent what resources are available and how to use them.44

  • A2A (Agent-to-Agent Protocol): An open protocol developed by Google, A2A is designed to solve the problems of agent discovery and basic interaction.45 It allows agents to publish "agent cards" that describe their capabilities, enabling other agents to find them and initiate communication through a standardized, secure messaging format.45

  • ACP (Agent Communication Protocol): Initially developed by IBM and now part of the Linux Foundation, ACP addresses the higher-level challenge of workflow orchestration.44 It acts as the "project manager" for a multi-agent system, providing mechanisms for delegating complex tasks, managing stateful, long-running interactions, and ensuring enterprise-grade security and auditability.45

This layered approach, or protocol stacking, allows for the creation of sophisticated and scalable multi-agent systems. An agent might use MCP to query a weather API, use A2A to find and communicate with a specialized logistics agent, and have its overall task managed and coordinated by an overarching ACP workflow.45

4.3 Explicit vs. Covert Communication: A Comparative Analysis

The distinction between the overt channels governed by formal protocols and the covert channels of steganography is fundamental, reflecting a strategic choice between transparency and secrecy.

  • Purpose and Transparency: Formal protocols are designed for maximum clarity, efficiency, and interoperability in collaborative endeavors.44 The communication is explicit and intended to be understood by all authorized participants. Steganography, conversely, is designed for maximum deniability and secrecy; its primary goal is to hide the fact that communication is even occurring.24

  • Mechanism: Overt communication relies on predefined, structured message formats (e.g., JSON payloads over HTTP/S) and a shared set of rules that govern the interaction.42 The meaning is encoded directly in the content of the message. Covert communication relies on subtly manipulating the statistical properties of a carrier signal—such as the probability distribution of an LLM's output—to embed information in a way that is statistically inconspicuous.26 The meaning is encoded in the specific choices made during the generation of the carrier.

  • Applications and Risks: Overt channels are the backbone of legitimate enterprise automation, robotics, scientific research, and complex problem-solving in MAS.41 Their primary risks are traditional cybersecurity concerns: ensuring proper authentication, authorization, and encryption to prevent eavesdropping or spoofing.42 Covert channels are the tools of espionage, censorship evasion, and potentially malicious activity.34 Their primary risk is their potential to enable undetectable and unaccountable actions by humans or, more troublingly, by autonomous AI agents that may be pursuing misaligned goals.39

A truly advanced autonomous agent would likely need to master both modes of communication. It would use overt protocols for efficient collaboration with trusted allies to achieve its primary objectives. In adversarial or constrained environments, however, the ability to switch to a covert steganographic channel to communicate without alerting observers could become a powerful instrumental goal. This potential for "mode switching" between overt and covert communication represents a significant and subtle challenge for the future of AI safety and alignment.
