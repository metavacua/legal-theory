Part II: The Rise of Modern Exchanges and the IP Framework

As the concept of the joint-stock company and the stock exchange proved successful, it was replicated across the globe. This period of formalization, which coincided with the creation of modern IP law in the U.S. Constitution, saw the standardization of market practices and the development of a concrete trail of intellectual property associated with the new corporations and exchanges.

Section 2.1: The IP of Modern Exchanges and Corporations

The model pioneered in Amsterdam was a catalyst for financial development worldwide. The London Stock Exchange was formally established in 1801, growing out of informal coffeehouse trading. In the United States, the Philadelphia Stock Exchange was founded in 1790, but it was the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE), born from the Buttonwood Agreement of 1792, that rose to prominence. These exchanges created organized marketplaces that standardized the process of trading. Unlike their predecessors, these new entities and the corporations they listed existed in a world with formal IP regimes, leading to the creation of protectable assets.

Copyrights in Corporate and Exchange Documents

From their inception, modern corporations and exchanges produced a variety of documents and tangible assets whose expression could be protected by copyright.

  • Stock Certificates: Beyond their function as proof of ownership, physical stock certificates became a medium for corporate branding and artistic expression. They were often elaborately printed with intricate designs, in part to thwart counterfeiters.5 These original artistic elements—the engravings, logos, and overall design—were copyrightable.3 For example, Disney became famous for designing stock certificates with full-color illustrations of its characters, turning them into collectible works of art.1 This practice created a secondary market for collectible certificates, a field known as scripophily, with some rare historical certificates selling for thousands of dollars.2 Today, companies that create custom replica stock certificates for display purposes explicitly state that their designs are protected by copyright.9

  • Exchange Rulebooks: The rules governing trading on an exchange are a form of literary work. Modern exchanges assert copyright over their rulebooks. For instance, the official rulebook of the New York Stock Exchange includes a copyright notice from NYSE Group, Inc., establishing its ownership over the text. The NYSE's terms of use further state that the text, content, and graphics contained in its services are protected by copyright.

  • Corporate Charters and Legal Filings: In contrast, foundational legal documents like a corporate charter (or Articles of Incorporation) are generally not protected by copyright. These documents are filed with a Secretary of State to legally establish the corporation and become part of the public record.10 As public records, they are not subject to the same copyright protections as privately created works.13 This distinction highlights an important practice for an IP-centric organization: separating copyrightable expressions from non-protectable public filings.

Trademarks for Exchanges and Brands

The establishment of formal exchanges created powerful new brands. The names and symbols of these institutions quickly became valuable assets, protected by trademark law. The NYSE and, later, Nasdaq built their reputations on these protected marks. These trademarks, such as NYSE® and NASDAQ®, are not just names; they are source identifiers that convey trust and liquidity to investors, and they are vigorously defended through licensing programs and litigation. Even the ticker symbols used to identify companies on an exchange can function as trademarks, serving as unique identifiers for a company's stock.

Section 2.2: The Regulatory Overlay: From the 1929 Crash to the SEC

The trajectory of American securities law was definitively shaped by market failure, not by a desire to reward financial innovation. The speculative frenzy of the 1920s, fueled by companies making promises with little substantive basis and brokers selling stock with minimal disclosure, culminated in the devastating stock market crash of 1929 and the subsequent Great Depression.

The U.S. Congress's response was not to create new property rights but to impose a comprehensive regulatory regime. This response established a clear legal path dependency away from IP and toward regulation for the conduct of securities sales. The landmark Securities Act of 1933 and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 became the bedrock of U.S. securities law.17 The core philosophy of these acts was not exclusion but

disclosure. Often called the "truth in securities" law, the 1933 Act's primary objectives were to require that investors receive significant financial and other information about securities offered for public sale and to prohibit deceit and fraud in the sale of those securities.13 It achieved this through a mandatory registration process, requiring companies to file detailed registration statements and prospectuses.17

The 1934 Act went a step further by creating the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), a powerful federal agency with broad authority to regulate the securities industry. The SEC was empowered to oversee exchanges, register and discipline broker-dealers, and require periodic reporting from publicly traded companies.13 This framework was built on top of pre-existing state-level regulations, known as "blue sky laws," which also aimed to combat fraud. The system was further augmented by the creation of Self-Regulatory Organizations (SROs), such as the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA), which are responsible for writing and enforcing rules of conduct for their members under SEC oversight.

This historical development reveals a conscious choice by the legal system. When faced with a crisis of market failure, the diagnosis was a problem of fraud, manipulation, and information asymmetry. The prescribed solution was a framework of regulation and mandatory disclosure designed to protect the public and restore trust. This approach cemented the view of securities markets as a kind of public utility to be regulated for the common good, operating in parallel with the IP laws that protected the specific technologies and creative works produced by market participants.
