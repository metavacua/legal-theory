Section V: Structural Models and Case Studies

To move from theoretical analysis to practical application, it is instructive to examine established and documented models that illustrate how these principles are applied in the real world. These cases demonstrate the spectrum of possibilities, from the most traditional structures to highly innovative capital-raising vehicles.

A. The Traditional Model: The Indivisible Membership

The most common cooperative structure, particularly for consumer and worker cooperatives, is the traditional, indivisible membership. In this model, all rights—use, control, and benefit—are bundled into a single membership share or certificate.1 Capital is raised through one-time membership fees and, most significantly, through retained patronage refunds (also known as capital credits). In this system, a portion of the annual surplus allocated to a member is retained by the cooperative as equity, with the promise of redemption at a future date.29 The membership itself is typically non-transferable and can only be redeemed by the cooperative, often upon the member leaving the co-op. This structure maximizes the integrity of the "closed loop," ensuring ownership and control remain exclusively with active patrons. However, it offers minimal liquidity and can be slow to accumulate the large amounts of capital needed for major projects.

B. The New Generation Cooperative (NGC) Model: Commodifying Patronage

The New Generation Cooperative, which emerged primarily in the agricultural sector, represents a major innovation in cooperative finance. The NGC commodifies the very right and obligation of patronage itself through the creation of tradable delivery rights.27 To join, a producer must make a significant upfront investment to purchase the right to deliver a specific quantity and quality of a commodity to the cooperative's value-added processing facility. This delivery right is a distinct, appreciable, and transferable asset.

This model brilliantly solves several problems. It raises substantial upfront capital to finance large-scale facilities. By making the delivery right an obligation as well as a right, it guarantees a stable supply of raw materials, allowing the plant to operate at peak efficiency.27 Crucially, it aligns with cooperative principles because the right is typically transferable only to other producers who can meet the delivery obligation, keeping ownership and control within the class of active patrons. The return on the investment comes from enhanced patronage—higher prices for the processed goods—not from a passive dividend. As analyzed previously, this structure is highly defensible under the "actually facilitates" test of Subchapter T.23

C. The External Capital Model: The Shared Capital Cooperative Case Study

Shared Capital Cooperative, a loan fund that lends to other cooperatives, provides a well-documented example of a Subchapter T cooperative that successfully raised external capital from non-patrons. Its SEC filing details a capital structure that includes multiple classes of stock, most notably Class A Preferred Stock sold to outside investors.20 This instrument is a quintessential "Subchapter T Security."

  • Structure: The Class A Preferred Stock is non-voting, explicitly separating financial rights from control rights.

  • Return: It offers a discretionary, non-cumulative dividend with a target rate of 3-6%, clearly subordinating the return on capital to the financial health and mission of the cooperative. The board, controlled by borrowing members, is not obligated to pay a dividend in any given year.

  • Compliance: This structure respects the three pillars. Democratic control is preserved with the patron-borrowers. Subordination of capital is achieved through the discretionary and capped dividend. The capital raised "actually facilitates" the cooperative's mission of providing loans to its members.

  • Securities Law: Shared Capital acknowledged the Class A Preferred Stock is a security and undertook a formal offering process, providing full disclosure to investors about the nature of their investment and its subordinated status.20 It also includes strict transfer restrictions, requiring board approval and giving the cooperative a right of first refusal, thereby preventing a secondary market from developing outside its control.

D. The Worker Co-op "Community Investor" Model

California law provides a unique statutory pathway for worker cooperatives to attract mission-aligned capital. California Corporations Code § 12253 and § 12404.5 create a special category for "community investors," defined as individuals or entities that invest in a worker cooperative but are not worker-members.13 This statute creates a specific, limited exception to the "one member, one vote" rule. It allows the articles or bylaws to grant these community investors voting rights, but their votes are subordinate to the worker-members. This model provides a legal framework for what is often called "multi-stakeholder" governance, allowing a cooperative to bring in capital from community allies who support its mission, while ensuring that ultimate control remains with the workers. This is a powerful, albeit narrowly targeted, tool for blending external capital with cooperative principles.
