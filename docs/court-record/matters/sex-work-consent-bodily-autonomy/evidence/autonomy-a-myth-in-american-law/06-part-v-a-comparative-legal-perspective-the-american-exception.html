Part V: A Comparative Legal Perspective: The American Exception

5.1. Contextualizing the American Model

The American system of contingent, precarious autonomy is not an inevitable feature of Western liberal democracy. Rather, it is a specific and exceptional choice. A comparative analysis with other common law and civil law nations reveals that the jurisprudential vacuum in the United States is a distinct feature of its legal architecture. Many other democracies have established explicit constitutional or statutory protections for personal autonomy, grounding it as a fundamental right rather than a subordinate privilege.

5.2. Explicit Protections Abroad

  • Canada: Section 7 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms guarantees the right to "life, liberty and security of the person".40 Canadian courts have interpreted this provision to protect a sphere of "personal autonomy involving inherently private choices" that are considered core to "individual dignity and independence".41 This protection extends to bodily integrity, the right to refuse medical treatment, and the freedom to make fundamental personal choices about one's life.40 While not absolute, any infringement by the state must be in accordance with the "principles of fundamental justice," a significantly higher bar than that which exists in U.S. law.44

  • Germany: Germany's Basic Law (Grundgesetz) provides a powerful and explicit constitutional foundation for personal autonomy. Article 1 declares that "Human dignity shall be inviolable," and Article 2 states that "Every person shall have the right to the free development of his personality... [and] the right to life and physical integrity".46 These articles are interpreted together to form a strong, general right of personality that robustly protects self-determination against state intrusion.49

  • United Kingdom: The Human Rights Act 1998 incorporates the European Convention on Human Rights into UK law.51 Article 8 of the Convention, which establishes the "Right to respect for private and family life," has been interpreted broadly by courts to protect a wide range of autonomous choices.52 This includes the right to determine one's lifestyle, sexual orientation, and appearance, as well as the right to control one's body and personal information.51 While this right can be limited, any interference by a public authority must be lawful and "necessary in a democratic society" for specific public interests, a standard that requires proportionality.52
